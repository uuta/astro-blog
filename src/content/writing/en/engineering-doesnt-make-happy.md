I was drinking at a bar after the gym. I know it's bad for my health. But I like the atmosphere of bars. Customers talk however they please, and the conversations that break out at random build up the buzz of the place from the bottom up. Nobody forces anyone; the conversations just spring up on their own, and they take on bold colors.

I glanced to the side and a stranger was drinking a Long Island Iced Tea. There'd been a half-finished glass left on the long table, so I'd assumed the seat was free. Wrong. A ten-seat long table, and a person right next to me. No way I couldn't talk to them now. "I didn't think anyone was here... Do you come here often?" That's how I opened.

Sadly, the conversation didn't take off. I got that they'd just started at an advertising company and were struggling with how to do the job. Lots of back-and-forth with clients, and this week seemed very busy. But I couldn't catch the finer nuances. I should have been catching about 50% of Al Jazeera in English, but I can't apply it to everyday conversation. I did try asking questions back to move the conversation along from my side, but I think it was all just safe, childish Q&A.

Things got kind of awkward, and I left the bar. But it was a strangely refreshing feeling. The joy of seeing someone react right in front of me, and the feeling of being disappointed in myself: I got both. How many days had I been dead, I wonder. Days of just sending pull requests, getting reviews, and branches getting merged before I noticed. Days of watching the overwhelming speed of my capable coworkers out of the corner of my eye, scared of being weeded out by the market. I have no say in decisions. Improving deploy speed and quality is the team's only mission. How is that any different from "a part-time job putting the plastic grass dividers into bento boxes"? The further downstream you are in the process, the more you have to kill your feelings. Engineering doesn't make people happy. For the last two years, I think I've just been dead.
