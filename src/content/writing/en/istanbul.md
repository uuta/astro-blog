The food is insanely good! The mosques are like Disneyland! So many cats, so cute! ☺️ Istanbul is a strange city where that cheerful first impression gets overturned in three days. Maybe my fate was sealed the moment the immigration officer tossed my passport back at me on arrival.



## A 3-meter radius - how many lies does it take to rip you off?

My impression is that a lot of people in Istanbul think within a 3 m radius. Cars that turn right even if it means blocking the flow of a straight road, people who cut into the line in the confusion even when you're queuing, merchants around Taksim Square looking to rip you off. They chase their own interest so hard that the utility of society as a whole goes down.

If we assume Japanese people are people who keep sacrificing themselves for the good of the whole, then Turkish people, I feel, are people who keep sacrificing society for their own good.

Is this the prisoner's dilemma...!?

### Story 1: I like Japonya!

When I went to eat with a Turkish woman I'd met on Bumble, a Turkish guy with a buzz cut, probably in his mid-20s, came to take our order. I couldn't really read the menu in either Turkish or English, so I tried to order a mojito, the only thing whose taste I could count on, and the waiter started talking to her in Turkish, on and on.

I was spacing out watching them, thinking they must be making small talk, Turks sure get along with each other, when the buzz cut turned to me, said "I like Japonya" and held out his hand. Of course I answered "I love Turkey!" with a bright face, but when I asked her later, he'd apparently been grilling her nonstop: "Where's this guy from?" "How old is he?" "Where are you from?" "How many people in your family?" "Where and how did you meet?"

Wait, what is he, a cop!?

Under that friendly-looking exchange in Turkish, sparks had apparently been flying. The buzz cut figured out I wasn't fluent in Turkish and ran an interrogation right in front of me. And then, "I like Japonya".

It was a moment that gave me a glimpse of everyday life where people lie as easily as they breathe.

### Story 2: The message that never gets read

It's a small thing, but this happened. The host of the Airbnb room I stayed in sent me a message on WhatsApp. They'd written me a review, so would I review them back? The room was very comfortable, I'll admit. But the Wi-Fi and the washing machine were broken, and it got in the way of my work, so I gave it four stars. I replied on WhatsApp, Reviewed! Thanks for the room!, and it still hasn't been read. What the hell.

### Story 3: What's the nice young man after...?

When I'd finished working at a café with power outlets near Galata Port in Istanbul, a kind-looking young man who seemed to be in his early 20s came up to me.

What he said was, "Your M1 Mac is so cool! I do the same kind of work." The Instagram he showed me had a few hundred followers and a handful of comments on his posts that friends seemed to have left casually, so it looked like he used it every day.

But he wanted to "go to another café, because I know a good place". Classic Istanbul. Lower my guard with a nice young man, then rip me off for a hundred-odd thousand yen at the bar we'd end up at together. But I can't let my Macbook Pro be taken hostage.

"Keep in touch"

I dodged him with the line a German CEO who made me work for free said to me a few years ago, and we parted.

## Movability is 💩

Istanbul's movability is 💩.

In several years of living the No house life, what I've found is that one of the conditions for a city that's easy to live in is movability. Is there a convenience store or supermarket for daily necessities and groceries within a 5-minute walk of your room, can you easily get to transport other than walking, is it safe: if conditions like these are met, a stay instantly becomes comfortable. For example, Asakusa in Tokyo is a ridiculously convenient neighborhood where everything is within a 1.5 km radius, and with the Tokyo Metro Ginza Line or the Asakusa Line you can easily get to the Chiba area and Tokyo's major districts.

Istanbul's main area, on the other hand, stretching from Taksim Square to Karaköy, is a bit drawn out. There are no convenience stores within a 2.5 km radius, and you have to trudge back and forth across hillsides that are mostly sloping streets (I realized later that the little shop I had no choice but to stop at had quietly ripped me off). You can get on the metro from Taksim Square, but Taksim Square itself is off at the edge, so it's a fairly painful job.

Could I live here? No way.

## Nobody speaks English

Apart from a few places, English doesn't get you anywhere. Ask in English and you get Turkish back. If you can't communicate by talking, life instantly gets harder. I couldn't even walk into a restaurant casually. It was the moment I felt how much my usual shaky English does for me. If you're going to Turkey, definitely learn some basic Turkish.

## You can't trust what's online

When I went, Istanbul was ranked 10th on NomadList. And if you search in Japanese for cafés with power outlets, it looks like nomads are working in more than enough of them.

But no...

Few cafés have outlets, and the cafés are far apart, so accessibility is bad. And there are far fewer nomads than you'd imagine, so you end up working feeling lonely. The comfortable-looking scene on the internet has been optimized through Google search.

Believe nothing, experience it...



## Europe isn't even looking at East Asia

The truth is, I struggled with myself right up until I published this. Because nothing but bad things about Istanbul came out of me. Two weeks of living with this "Turkey loves Japan" idea, something I couldn't feel one bit on the ground. Like Istanbul's gloomy, cloudy sky, my heart wore out.

But now that some time has passed, I can say it. Istanbul is shit after all.

There's still hope, though, because there's a world I haven't seen. Uncle Abzel, who invited me for tea at a shop I wandered into to buy water, and a guy at a kebab place that served rice who chatted with me in a friendly way, like a kindly old cartoon baker. It's also true that I saw a faint hope that, if only I knew the language, the conversation could open up. If you're going to Turkey, Turkish is a must. Learn about 100 words before you go.



On this European expedition (Berlin, Istanbul), which I went on in the red, I got burned. I feel like the bullied kid. Europe isn't even looking at East Asia! If you're going alone, go like your life depends on it. I respect your guts.
