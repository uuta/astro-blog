Misaki, a friend of many years, turned 61. She told me she plans to have a child with an artificial womb. The artificial womb, first put into practical use and written into law in China eight years ago, made it possible to have a child without it growing inside the mother's body. At first, many people here raised concerns on ethical grounds. Wouldn't it lead to human trafficking? Hadn't science reached territory it must not enter? "Outsourcing" children seemed like a challenge to what is natural.

On the other hand, there were plenty of voices in favor. First, avoiding pregnancy protects the mother's safety. In the past, a few dozen women died every year during pregnancy or childbirth. Compared with the number of pregnant women, the death rate was as close to zero as it gets, but still, someone died. And the whole process of pregnancy, birth and child-rearing is an enormous burden to begin with. Many women wanted to have children the easy way, without the morning sickness during pregnancy, the pain of labor, or a C-section. Being able to have a child regardless of sex or age also gave many people hope. Especially for married or unmarried couples who were older, going through fertility treatment, or gay, it was pure hope. It meant an era in which anyone could have a child, freed from the limits of the body. Surprisingly, a lot of single women and men supported the artificial womb. Spend your time on yourself while your body is in good shape. Think about raising a child once age starts wearing you down. With the arrival of the artificial womb, the idea of a "marriageable age" faded, and marriage became even more of an empty shell in meaning and presence. The age of parents in each household inevitably went up. Pushed along by public opinion, the government stepped in too. Drawing on the legal systems of the United States and other countries, it went ahead with amendments to the Civil Code and the Penal Code so that children could be born from artificial wombs, and enacted the "Ex Utero Pregnancy Act". For a government that couldn't come up with any fundamental answer to the falling birth rate, the artificial womb was worth its weight in gold. And so the artificial womb, humanity's maverick child, quietly carved itself into people's minds.

Misaki says she's in the middle of looking for the ideal sperm on "Childs". Childs, run by the Israeli startup "Nexir", is an app that matches human sperm and eggs. Register sperm or eggs on Childs, and it measures their potential and shows you a score made up of 300 variables. If you find sperm or eggs from a partner you like, you can buy them and request that a fertilized egg be grown. That's the first step. Next, Childs uses artificial intelligence to predict the future face, athletic ability, intelligence and lifespan from the embryo after fertilization. Within two months of fertilization, the user has to decide whether to raise it. If you can commit, you submit a notice of expected birth to the authorities and wait for the day it's born. And if you can't find the courage, the fertilized egg is automatically discarded. Opinions are divided on whether it's acceptable to discard fertilized eggs, but Childs may be more ethical than what came before. Under current law, the Maternal Health Act allows abortion up to the 22nd week of pregnancy, but Childs allows only up to two months. In other words, it demands a decision earlier than the period in which a pregnant woman can have an abortion.

The reason Childs is making such big profits lies in its distinctive business model. When a user buys, a fixed amount is paid to the person who provided the sperm or egg. That amount is always shifting with supply and demand. For example, the going rate for an ordinary user's sperm is 3,000-5,000 yen and for eggs 50,000-200,000 yen, while the CEO of a famous company, a well-known model or an athlete can command a premium big enough to buy a house. Providing sperm and eggs has become fully commercial. Childs books a monthly fee at sign-up and a 15% margin on each successful match as revenue, and it's growing fast.

Misaki says she has already tried fertilizing several of the eggs she'd had frozen. It's a trick you can only try because sperm is relatively cheap. She compares several fertilized eggs and picks the one with the best simulation results. Comparing options side by side is something everyone does, Misaki says. "I looked at the simulation results after fertilization, but none of the numbers really satisfied me. Maybe I'm just making excuses, though. I do want one within a year."
